## Main Content

HM414 (Open Elective, Winter 2025-26)

A Beginner’s Introduction to the Psyche

Instructor: Dr. Jenson Joseph

Course format: 3 hours of lectures (3-0-0-3) 
Pre-requisites: HSS core courses

Course outline:

There are two primary approaches to the entity at the centre of this course — the psyche. 
One understands it as the site that constitutes human subjectivity, imbuing our 
experience of life and the world with meaning and value. The other views it as the site of 
pathological issues. These approaches have generated two distinct intellectual discourses 
on the workings of the psyche: psychoanalysis and psychology. While the latter is largely 
a clinical practice focused on helping people cope with the pathologies of the mind, 
psychoanalysis has evolved as a powerful explanatory and interpretative framework, 
offering a theory of the psyche as a generative domain through which virtually all aspects 
of life — and the social, cultural, and political formations arising from it — can be 
understood. This course introduces foundational concepts from these disciplines 
through a discussion of the seminal theories proposed by Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) 
and Jacques Lacan (1901–1981).

We will explore Freud’s theories of the unconscious, the pleasure principle, the Oedipus 
complex, and the triadic structure of the id, ego, and superego. We will also examine 
Lacan’s concepts of the Mirror Stage, alienation, and his schema of the Symbolic, 
Imaginary, and the Real. Cultural and media texts, literary works, and discussions of 
current events will be used to introduce these complex concepts and help students 
develop a basic understanding of the human psyche, its operations, and why it matters to 
study them. The course will also delve into methods of applying the psychoanalytic 
framework to evolve understandings and interpretations of socio-political and cultural 
phenomena, especially when they involve matters of the spirit. These discussions will 
build toward a thematic focus on socio-cultural transformations in the age of algorithms 
and artificial intelligence, examined from the vantage point of their impact on the psyche 
and on the constitution of the modern subject, in contrast to the era of industrial 
capitalism in which classical psychoanalytic theories were devised.

Course Outcome: At the basic level, this course is meant to encourage students to

develop an interest in key concepts in the discourses on the human psyche by exploring 
immediately relatable questions such as: Why do human beings experience adult life as a 
landscape of complex emotions and inner conflicts that defy rational explanation? Why

do we struggle to control, express, or even recognize our desires? What is the origin of 
our dreams? What makes us dependent on others or objects, sometimes to our own 
detriment? Why is “madness” a uniquely human condition—seemingly tied to our 
capacity for reason? Why do we often feel we lack full control over our will and 
consciousness, as though an alien force resides within us?

By helping students develop perspectives that enable them to engage such questions, the 
course equips them to understand the human psyche as a powerful faculty whose 
operations produce both meaning and value as well as potential pathologies. In an age 
when self-care and self-management are fetishized as pathways to well-being, this course 
encourages students to reflect on the self, society, and the structures that shape psychic 
formations. For participants who show particular interest, the course will also introduce 
procedures for interpreting socio-cultural phenomena from a psychoanalytic perspective.
