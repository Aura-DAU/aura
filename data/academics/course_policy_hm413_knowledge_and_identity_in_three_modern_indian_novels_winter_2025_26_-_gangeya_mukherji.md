# HM413 Knowledge and Identity in Three Modern Indian Novels Winter 2025 26

## Main Content

Dhirubhai Ambani University
Elective Course: HM 413: Knowledge and Identity in Three Modern Indian Novels
An Open Elective Course for the Senior Undergraduate/Postgraduate Students in
the School of Technology
Course Format: 3 Credits. 3 Lectures per Week
Prerequisites: An interest in the question of Identity, the traditional knowledge systems in India,
along with some literary sensibility in the minds of the applicants for this course.
Course Content: This course has been visualised for undergraduate students without an
intermediate academic background in the liberal arts stream. It aims to encourage and
contextualize some of the natural questions that might arise in a young mind regarding
individual identity, as framed in Indian traditions and knowledge systems.
This course belongs to the academic category of the Indian Knowledge System (IKS).
This course will examine the concept of individual/communitarian identity within the Indian
Knowledge System, which originated in the Indian subcontinent and evolved over time
through interactions with other schools of thought, facilitated by political and cultural
exchanges spanning two thousand years. This question of identity and knowledge systems
will be explored through its depiction in three iconic modern Indian novels:
Saraswatichandra, Gora, and Aag Ka Dariya (River of Fire), written by Govardhanram Tripathi
in Gujarati, Rabindranath Tagore in Bengali, and Qurratalain Hyder in Urdu, respectively. The
course shall engage with their English translations, but students are encouraged to read
them in their original versions depending on their competency in Gujarati, Bangla, and Urdu.
It is essential to note that the course will not focus on a close reading of the entire body of
the texts to be studied, especially given the considerable length of the texts in question. The
course will focus on the theme of the course as explored in the three novels rather than on
the study of the literary aspects of the novels in their entirety.
Within this broad framework of the Indian Knowledge system, this course, visualised around
the concept of Identity as imagined and defined in Indian knowledge system, aims to
understand their relevance, as well as their outline, substance, and significance in a fast-
changing present, and in the Indian context particularly, their epistemic position in the
context of representation and participation across genders, classes and communities. The
course also incorporates concepts such as truth, conscience, violence/non-violence,
responsibility, and deprivation, from the perception of their specific natures in Indian

LEARNING OUTCOMES: The course aims to acquaint students with the formative themes that these
three thinkers/authors have explored and contributed to the modern concept of India, and to
identify their central arguments about identity as defined within the Indian knowledge system.
Students are encouraged to raise their individual questions regarding the art and arguments of
these three authors.
COURSE REQUIREMENTS
Attendance and Participation: A minimum attendance rate of 60% is compulsory. The
attendance policy will be strictly enforced, and students who fall short of the required
attendance percentile will be subject to a grade reduction in accordance with the attendance
policy. The success of the course depends significantly on students' engagement with the
readings and with one another in class.
Mode of Teaching: The course will be organized around the concept of Indian identity as
explored by the three authors chosen for study, and divided into four broad modules. The first
module will consist of drawing the background of the course, identifying the main strands of
thought emerging in modern India within the broad intellectual context of the meeting of
cultures that took place in the Indian subcontinent in the preceding centuries, and defining the
characteristics of Indian traditions and their knowledge system. The remaining three modules
will be thematically centred around the three novels, respectively. The lectures will involve
engagement with the themes, along with readings specifically focused on the central theme of
the course.
EVALUATION: The first evaluation, in the form of multiple-choice questions covering the first
three modules, and accounting for 30% of the total marks, will be conducted as part of the
Second In-Semester examinations. Group presentations, to be made by students, will be
scheduled during the last phase of lectures and will account for an additional 10% of the marks.
Students are encouraged to make presentations based on their personal interpretations of the
theme and content of the novels or create scenarios that relate to the themes of the three
novels. The End-of-Semester examination, comprising multiple-choice, short-answer, and one
detailed-answer question, will cover the entire course and account for 60% of the marks.

PLAGIARISM AND ACADEMIC INTEGRITY: Using words and phrases of authors without proper citation is
considered a violation of norms of academic integrity and is strictly discouraged. Genuine effort
and commitment will be duly appreciated.
COURSE OUTCOMES: The course aims to achieve the following outcomes:
• Introduction to Indian Knowledge System.
• Understanding the ethical human responsibility for nurturing a peaceful comity of
races and communities.
• The novels to be studied reflect an understanding of knowledge as a repository,
imagination, introspection, and evolution, and identity as a combination of a
historical core and reflective process evolving through advancing knowledge arising
from interaction among human communities. This concept of knowledge and identity
forms an intrinsic part of Indian knowledge systems. Scholars and thinkers in India
generally envisioned a universe intricately connected in both its creation and
destruction. Therefore, all human activity was, in some way or other, part of this
quest to discover the self and the world. Each individual developed with the support
of others, whether it was visible or not.
• Knowing and understanding this fact was one of the highest functions of human
consciousness. One should celebrate one’s heritage, but should remain mindful of its
universal human characteristics. Awareness of this fact is best reflected in the
transcendence of chauvinism and intolerance, as well as in sensitivity to global ecological
concerns, the commonality of concerns, and transnational moral claims to basic amenities
across cultures. The course will highlight the importance of this formative and
pervasive principle of life.
