## Course Description

This course is an introduction, within a social scientific framework, to Indian social organization with special reference to its social forms and their genealogies. The course begins by foregrounding the emergence of India as a civilizational imaginary and nation-state within the narrative-historical framework of the region. The aim is to unpack the idea of the "unity" of India given the pluralism and diversity it ostensibly accommodates.

Historically, India has been interpreted as a nation of communities with well-established communal ways of living, reflected in its texts, habits of life, traditions, agrarian practices, social organization, political behaviour, and other aspects of social life. However, the advent of modernity has profoundly changed South Asian society. Colonial modernity has catalyzed socially and politically important changes in its social fabric, raising important questions: Was India always a region with fixed boundaries and identity, a stable and constant social organization, and settled communal modes of life? In what ways was pre-modern India different from the present one? What sort of changes did colonialism bring about in Indian society? Is there an inherent civilizational dynamic to social life in India markedly different from that found in Western societies? Did the nation-state India flatten out all of its myriad forms of diversity — communities, languages, cultures, lifestyles — and their historical origins? Or did the social forms in India find new modes of articulation in conditions of modernity? How is the history of the present to be understood, given what happened in South Asia in the last few centuries?

The course aims to find answers to these questions with the help of decades-long social scientific scholarship. It covers several relevant topics, such as colonial law and state, the formation of the public, colonial knowledge, village India, agrarian economy, peasant histories, caste and social structure, modernization, family and kinship, nationalism, communalism, and globalization.

**Course Objective:** This course aims to construct a comparative framework for understanding different cultures with particular reference to social organization, politics, religion, and symbolism, illustrated with various ethnographic examples. The course is designed to provide the means to apply basic anthropological understandings of society and culture in the analysis of meanings, actions, and explanations that form the basis for communication in society. Students will be expected to reflect upon Indian society utilizing the readings and lectures.

## Textbooks and References

### Reading List

1. John F. Richards, "Early Modern India and World History."
2. Bernard Cohn, "Notes on the History of the Study of Indian Society and Culture."
3. Barbara Metcalf and Thomas Metcalf, "The East India Company Raj: 1772-1850," from *A Concise History of Modern India*.
4. Milton Singer, "Beyond Tradition and Modernity in Colonial Madras."
5. Joseph R. Gusfield, "Tradition and Modernity: Misplaced Polarities in the Study of Social Change," *American Journal of Sociology*, Vol. 72, No. 4 (Jan. 1967), pp. 351-362.
6. Bernard Cohn, "From Indian Status to British Contract."
7. David Ludden, "Modernity," from *An Agrarian History of South Asia*.
8. Benedict Anderson, "The Origins of National Consciousness," excerpt from *Imagined Communities*.
9. Clifford Geertz, "Balinese Cockfight" (referenced by author/topic).
10. "Seven Prevalent Misconceptions about India's Caste System," in Diane P. Mines and Sarah E. Lamb (eds.), *Everyday Life in South Asia*.
11. S. Anandhi, "Women's Question in the Dravidian Movement, c. 1925-1948."
12. "Anti-Arrack Agitation of Women in Andhra Pradesh," 4 March 2024.
