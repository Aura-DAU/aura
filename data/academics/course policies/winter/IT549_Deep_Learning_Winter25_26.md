## Course Description

Deep Learning aims to equip students with a comprehensive understanding of the principles, algorithms, and applications of neural networks and deep learning techniques. Through theoretical knowledge and practical experience, students will gain the expertise to effectively design, implement, and deploy neural network-based solutions across various domains. The course emphasizes practical application development through course projects and lab sessions, preparing students to understand and implement deep learning algorithms and methodologies.

---

## Course Outcomes (COs)

- Understand the advantages of deep learning.
- Gain in-depth knowledge of advanced architectures, including CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, Attention mechanisms, Transformers, and Autoencoders (at a high level).
- Theoretical and practical understanding of the architectures and insights into design choices for better model development.
- Learn essential model training concepts, including regularization, dropout, data augmentation, batch normalization, and hyperparameter tuning, which are explored for effective optimization.
- Hands-on demos using TensorFlow/PyTorch on images, text, time series, language data, etc., are included for all architectures, equipping students with practical skills to excel in deep learning.

## Course Structure

| Module | Topic | No. of Lectures |
|---|---|---|
| Module 1: Foundations of Deep Learning and Neural Computation | Algebraic, Probabilistic, and Graphical View of Data; Importance of deep learning, representation learning from large datasets, case studies & modern applications; artificial neurons, inputs, weights, and activation functions; relationship with linear models; multilayer neural networks and matrix–vector operations; input–hidden–output layers; motivation for deeper architectures | 6 |
| Module 2: Training Neural Networks — Backpropagation & Optimisation | Hyperparameters vs. parameters; supervised training pipeline; loss functions; detailed mathematics of backpropagation; computational graph perspective; training challenges; vanishing/exploding gradients; batch normalisation; improved optimisers (SGD variants, momentum, Adam); learning-rate schedules; initialisation strategies (Xavier/He) | 6 |
| Module 3: Regularisation, Generalisation & Practical Training Strategies | Overfitting and underfitting; reducing model size; L1/L2 regularisation; dropout; early stopping; data augmentation; transfer learning; unsupervised pre-training; pre-training auxiliary tasks; fine-tuning strategies for modern architectures | 6 |
| Module 4: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) | Motivation; convolution operation; images as rank-3 tensors; filters/kernels; strides, padding, dilation; feature maps and receptive fields; stacking layers; pooling layers and their varieties; modern CNN architectures (LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet — conceptual, not detailed blueprints); training CNNs, hyperparameters, memory constraints; overfitting in vision; data augmentation; pre-trained CNNs; transfer learning in vision tasks | 6 |
| Module 5: Recurrent Models for Sequences — RNN, LSTM, GRU | Recurrent neurons and layers; unfolding through time; training RNNs; time-series forecasting with simple and deep RNNs; vanishing/exploding gradients in sequences; LSTM and GRU architectures; forecasting and sequence modeling examples | 6 |
| Module 6: Sequence-to-Sequence Models & Attention Mechanisms | Encoder–decoder networks; bidirectional RNNs; teacher forcing & scheduled sampling; beam search; attention mechanism (intuition and mathematics); self-attention; comparison of recurrent vs. attention-based architectures; positional encodings | 6 |
| Module 7: Transformer Architecture and Modern Deep Learning Paradigms | The Transformer architecture, multi-head attention, feed-forward blocks, residual connections, training considerations (batching, masking, optimisers), pre-training and fine-tuning large models, and a conceptual overview of Vision Transformers (ViT) and GPT-style models | 6 |
| **Total** | | **42** |

*Note: A separate section of the source document states "Challenges and Course Project Viva [20% + 25% = 45%]," which does not exactly match the 30%+15%=45% figures given in the Evaluation Scheme table above (both sum to 45% overall, but the individual Challenges/Project Viva split differs between the two places in the source document — this discrepancy is preserved faithfully from the source).*

**Challenges (Team-based):**
- Students form groups of two for 100-minute ML development challenges (three challenges during lab hours across the semester).
- Team registration opens the second week after classes start; groups must register via Google Form within three days of the announcement. Teams remain unchanged for the semester once registered.
- The three most proficient teams in each challenge receive a bonus of up to 3% on their respective scores.

**Course Project [25%]:**
- One project: an End-to-End ML application (full-stack — front-end and back-end, build and deploy) solving a data-driven problem with high social impact.
- Each group selects a thematic domain (Finance, E-commerce, Healthcare, Pharma, Sports, Entertainment, Renewable Energy, Oil & Gas, Automobile, Agriculture, FMCG, Cybersecurity, Social Media, Supply Chain, Manufacturing, Business Automation, or another socially-aligned domain).
- Each group defines the problem, collects dataset(s) from reliable sources (no two groups may use the same dataset), and must add a novel contribution compared to existing baselines.
- Submission: project code repository with a comprehensive README on GitHub Classroom.
- Evaluation is primarily a "Chalk Talk" with the instructor; any student may be asked questions about any aspect of the project.
- Late submissions (up to 24 hours) incur a 20% penalty.
