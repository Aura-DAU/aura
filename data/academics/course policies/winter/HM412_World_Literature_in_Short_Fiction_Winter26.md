## Course Description

"The experience of reading world literature is a form of travel—a mode of cultural encounter that presents us with languages, cultural norms, customs and ideas that may be unfamiliar to us, even strange. As readers, each time we begin to read a new work, we put ourselves in the role of a traveller in a foreign land, trying to understand its practices and values and hoping to feel, to some degree and in some way, connected to and welcome among the people we meet there" (*The Norton Anthology of World Literature*).

Following an introductory discourse on the history and overarching tradition of world literature, this course limits its scope to texts produced during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. This period of world history bore witness to radical change and profound upheaval, marked by world wars, the fall of empires, the rise of new ideologies, and unprecedented technological advancements. This course explores how authors across the globe utilised the short story form to capture the anxieties and transformations of the world around them. The readings are organised into four thematic modules that position the 'modern individual' against the bureaucratic city, the legacy of the empire, linguistic labyrinths, and the frailty of borders. Through this eclectic mix of narratives, learners are expected to cultivate a deeper appreciation for the short story form as a global medium of artistic expression.

*Note: This course demands rigorous reading. Students will be required to read all the primary texts. Furthermore, the attendance benchmark for this course is 60%; attendance below this threshold will result in a reduction of the grade.*

**Course Objectives:**
- Situate key works of short fiction within their broader historical and cultural contexts.
- Explore the development of major literary movements through representative texts.
- Cultivate skills in close reading and critical analysis.
- Present a diverse selection of short fiction from various global traditions to highlight the richness of modern world literature.

## Textbooks and References

### Introduction

- David Damrosch, "Goethe Coins a Phrase," in *What Is World Literature* (2003).

### Primary Readings

**Module One: Modernity and the Bureaucratic City**
1. Nikolai Gogol, "The Overcoat" (Russia, 1842).
2. Herman Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (USA, 1853).

**Module Two: Empire and the Private Sphere**
3. Rabindranath Tagore, "Punishment" (India, 1892).
4. Katherine Mansfield, "The Garden Party" (New Zealand, 1922).
5. Doris Lessing, "The Old Chief Mshlanga" (Southern Rhodesia/Zimbabwe, 1951).

**Module Three: Labyrinths of Meaning – Language and Translation**
6. Ryūnosuke Akutagawa, "In a Bamboo Grove" (Japan, 1922).
7. Jorge Luis Borges, "The Garden of Forking Paths" (Argentina, 1941).
8. Vladimir Nabokov, "Signs and Symbols" (USA/Russia, 1948).

**Module Four: Revolution, Partition, and the Border**
9. Lu Xun, "Diary of a Madman" (China, 1918).
10. Saadat Hasan Manto, "Toba Tek Singh" (India, 1955).
11. Albert Camus, "The Guest" (France/Algeria, 1957).
