## Course Description

The gripping heroic narrative of the Mahabharata also contains a vast number of subsidiary stories and discussions, which together reflect the entire range of human emotions and situations, while debating the nature of right and wrong, both in the context of immediate human situations and the overarching philosophy of life. This essential debate over right and wrong, presented in different forms within the epic — above all in the Bhagavad Gita — might be the main reason for the epic to be regarded variously as a treatise on ethics and metaphysics (dharmashastra), a repository of the highest knowledge (the fifth veda), a guide to right action (itihasa), and the human condition itself.

The discussions and episodes of the epic primarily involve the question of the ultimate goal of human life, emphasised in its closing section: the question of right and wrong. The narrative contains episodes so acute and familiar as to evoke sympathy for seemingly immoral characters, raising the question of whether the Mahabharata is a moral text at all. The course explores dharma as both means and end in the Mahabharata's philosophical universe, and argues that the epic does not advocate withdrawal from life but participation in it according to the norms of dharma, without undue attachment to sensory experience.

The course also examines the complex and problematic nature of "desire" as the mental impulse behind action, and traces how scholars believe the Bhrigu clan, as custodians of the epic's oral tradition, incorporated the subjects of dharma (law) and niti (conduct) prominently into the Santi and Anushasana parvas.

The course will involve detailed readings of some chapters of the Mahabharata and Bhagavad Gita, along with discussion and presentation of the fundamental questions in those chapters regarding right and wrong. The course will concentrate mainly on the following topics:

- The main features of the Narration of the Mahabharata
- The story of the epic
- Certain complicated ethical issues, such as the destiny of Karna; the anxiety of Amba/Shikhandi; the Injustice to Ekalavya; the marriage of Draupadi; and the episode of the game of dice
- The short conversation between Yudhishthira and the Yaksha
- The theme of the Bhagavad Gita
- The imagery of the two trees – those of Dharma and Adharma
- The Ethical Lapses of the Mahabharata War
- The question of Non-Violence in the Mahabharata
- Pain, Truth and Eternal Relevance of the Mahabharata
- The Final Journey of the Pandavas from Earth

## Course Outcomes (COs)

**Learning Outcomes:** The course aims to acquaint students with the narrative and themes of the Mahabharata and invites them to think critically about the complex issues of pain, untruth, violence, compassion, and equanimity. It will also familiarise them with different approaches to reading texts by examining various textual devices, identifying their central arguments, and raising questions that may arise from individual perspectives.

**Additional Course Outcomes:**

| CO | Description |
|---|---|
| CO1 | Understand the broader ethical, human responsibility of overriding importance while approaching scientific and technical challenges relating to building amenities and individual and corporate wealth. |
| CO2 | Correlate concepts from the Indian tradition of ethics and argument with the frequent challenges raised to it by modern discourses on entitlement, equality, hierarchy, exclusion, and power (with limited, non-exhaustive correlation to western philosophy). |
| CO3 | Develop a deep understanding of life's complicated realities, and regard life as a continuous dedication to ethical purpose that is simultaneously individual and collective. |
| CO4 | Appreciate the demanding and exciting exercise of distinguishing between the correct choice and the merely attractive one, and the importance of due diligence in acting on the proper course of action in various situations of material existence. |

## Additional Notes

- **Attendance and Participation:** The success of the course depends significantly on students' engagement with the readings and with one another in class.
- **Mode of Teaching:** The course is divided into three broad modules as described in the Course Structure section above.
- **Plagiarism and Academic Integrity:** Using words and phrases of authors without proper citation is considered a violation of norms of academic integrity and is strictly discouraged. Genuine effort and commitment will be duly appreciated.
- The course explicitly notes it is not an exercise in philosophy in general, or western philosophy in particular, but is centred on a particular Indian tradition of ethics and argument, though some correlation with western philosophy concepts may be made without constituting an extensive exercise.
- The instructor, Gangeya Mukherji, has authored/co-edited one of the recommended reference readings on the Mahabharata's ethical dimensions.
