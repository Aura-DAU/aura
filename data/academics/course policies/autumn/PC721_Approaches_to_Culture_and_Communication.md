## Course Description

Communication between any form of transmitter and recipient is not just a matter of 'hardware' and 'software.' Communication — what it achieves, how it is interpreted, what is understood, conveyed and represented — involves social ideology, cultural techniques, and a whole society's historical context. After all, communication does not take place in a vacuum! By the 18th century, mainly in the western world, the development of print culture led to the creation and evolution of a modern 'public culture.' This included the process of identifying, weighing, and discussing the issues of the day in ways hardly possible before the print revolution.

This multidisciplinary seminar course allows students to explore the interrelationship of orality, writing, printing, the visual image, theatre, and music, by encouraging students to research and discuss the interaction between various modalities of communication within Indian society and history. The course intends to examine how culture communicates and communication creates culture, with particular intent to consider the relationship between image and sound.

This course is based on a seminar format. It is aimed at developing conceptual schemes and patterns by which historically (across changing times — including the future and its base in the past) ideas, objects, and actions have been developed and/or evolved to communicate within Indian culture and its contextual positioning with other cultures as broad as colonialism and globalization. This may include broadly: language socialization, literacy, music and the visual arts, the power of language, miscommunication, and universal and culture-specific properties of human communication as embodied in ritual performances, organized spectacles, and structured events in public spaces.

Utilizing ideas from Anthropology, Sociology, Semiotics, History, and Cultural Studies, the course first provides an understanding of basic conceptual and analytical foundations. Actual context and its depiction (ethnographic case) will be presented along with analytical frameworks that can be derived from it (theoretical).

The course expects students to be open-minded, critical, and to think about society and culture beyond India. Students are expected to acquire basic sensibility to various assumptions about how and why societies communicate. Readings for each section will be dealt with in class as discussion sessions with student participation and presentation. Lectures are not solely based on the readings; readings serve as a base to back up ideas presented by faculty.

**Course Content and Themes:** With globalization, the Internet, and the general shrinking of time and space, cross-cultural interaction has become a necessity in people's everyday lives. This course is designed to interrogate different aspects of cross-cultural communication and cultural differences: family life, social relationships, the workplace, government, education, gender, romance, and religion. Throughout exploring these topics, the course strives to engage in personal self-reflection, hands-on experience, and understanding connections to larger social structures.

**Specific goals of this course:**
1. To describe, learn, and see common and important patterned social and cultural differences.
2. To provide a space for students to reflect on their own personal experience with cultural difference.
3. To encourage students to engage with different cultures in a hands-on way and to meaningfully experience cross-cultural communication.

## Evaluation / Grading Scheme

| Component | Weightage |
|---|---|
| Final Project and Presentation | 55% |
| Class Participation, Assignments, Presentations, and Pop Quiz | 45% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

**Evaluation approach:** Continuous evaluation and a research project.
- Students are expected to attend lectures, participate in all discussions, and undertake assignments based on readings, above and beyond the provided references. Active and informed participation impacts the grade.
- Sequence of lectures covers essential ideas the course intends to impart, followed by discussion around readings to understand basic ideas in relation to concrete, specific cultural cases.
- The "research project" must be submitted by each individual student as a researched paper (double-spaced, typed, 10–12 pages, 12pt font, normal margins), focusing on a theme provided by faculty. Students are expected to consult course coordinators to develop their research topic. This project must also be presented individually in the classroom.

## Additional Notes

**Honour Code:** Academic dishonesty — cheating, plagiarism, or any kind of deceit — will not be tolerated and will result in a zero for the assignment. All ideas and words not self-generated must be cited. In extreme cases, a failing grade may be assigned for the course or a specific assignment.

**Study Tip (as stated in source document):** "Learn to take lecture notes and keep a small notebook to write ideas and words that you do not understand and look up the meaning or ask in class. But make a note where the question originated from."

- This is a heavily reading- and discussion-based seminar course, with an extensive interdisciplinary reading list spanning visual anthropology, semiotics, and cultural studies.
- The course places strong emphasis on ethnographic case studies (e.g., Rathwa Pithoro painting, Andamanese photography/clay painting, Kutch art and architecture) alongside theoretical frameworks.
