## Course Description

This is a core humanities/social sciences course which includes elements of both microeconomics and macroeconomics. It aims to develop an understanding of basic concepts of resource allocation, opportunity costs, and market failure. Students would also get an introduction to macroeconomic concepts such as national income accounting, unemployment, and inflation. References to monetary, fiscal, and foreign exchange rate policies will help students gain a greater understanding of the role of central banks and governments in the economy. The course will attempt to engage students in debates about public policy through an economic lens.

## Course Outcomes (COs)

After completion of the course, students should be able to:

| CO | Description |
|---|---|
| CO1 | Identify and distinguish between real-world examples of different types of markets, including monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and perfect competition |
| CO2 | Analyse market efficiency, consumer surplus, producer surplus, and social welfare in economic markets |
| CO3 | Recognise macroeconomic principles behind monetary and fiscal policies |
| CO4 | Recognize and interpret the differences between concepts such as real vs. nominal GDP; value added method vs. expenditure/income methods, etc. |

## Course Structure

*Note: The source document frames this as a "Suggested Lecture Distribution" and notes: "The table below gives the suggested distribution for 36 (out of ~42 lectures). The coverage of remaining topics will be at the discretion of the concerned faculty."*

| Unit | Topic | No. of Lectures |
|---|---|---|
| Microeconomics: Basic Concepts | Principles of economics/basic concepts (scarcity, opportunity costs, incentives) | 2 |
| Microeconomics: Demand & Supply | Demand, supply and equilibrium; elasticity of demand/elasticity of supply | 6 |
| Microeconomics: Welfare Analysis | Producer surplus, consumer surplus | 3 |
| Microeconomics: Market Failure | Market failure, externalities and role of the government: price floor/ceiling, taxes and deadweight loss. Additional discussion: pollution, climate change (linking with market failure and externalities) | 6 |
| Microeconomics: Market Structures | Non-technical (without graphs or math) overview of market structures: perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition, focusing on their distinct features and connection with real-world phenomena such as rent-seeking, price discrimination, etc. Additional discussion: capitalism and market power | 2 |
| Introducing the Macroeconomy | National income accounting, basic terms/vocabulary (GDP, inflation, etc.). Additional discussion: poverty and inequality | 3 |
| Macroeconomics: Fiscal Policy | Budget, deficits, taxation | 3 |
| Macroeconomics: Monetary Policy | Money and banking, credit creation, role of the central bank | 4 |
| Macroeconomics: International Economics | Trade & tariffs, determination of exchange rates. Additional discussion: economic imperialism, neocolonialism | 5 |
| Case Study | Global financial crisis (or any recent economic event) | 2 |
| **Total** | | **36** |

## Additional Notes

- The source document title itself reads "MC314/HM116: Principles of Economics," indicating the course carries a dual/cross-listed code; the filename designates this file's primary code as HM116.
- **Guidance for instructors (as stated in source)**: This is a first course in economics, with almost no calculus or heavy math. The focus is on building economic intuition and encouraging students to "think like an economist," rather than introducing mathematically involved models of optimization or macroeconomic theories. The intention is to equip students with sufficient understanding of economic principles to grasp the links between economics, society, and policymaking, enabling students to connect economics with other HSS courses and discuss real-world problems such as poverty, inequality, and climate change.
