## Course Description

This course is intended for undergraduate students without an intermediate academic background in the liberal arts stream. It aims to encourage and contextualize natural questions that might arise in a young mind regarding individual identity, framed in ethical concepts such as truth, community living, and the individual duty to contribute to the preservation and growth of material and intellectual ecology, broadly defined as public service.

The course falls under the rubric of intellectual history (history of ideas). Within this broad framework, it examines the origins of the concept of India in 19th-century India, set against a complex web of national and international currents, with a particular focus on Vivekananda, Tagore, and Gandhi. It incorporates concepts such as truth, conscience, violence/non-violence, responsibility, deprivation, suppression, and cultural issues, emerging from their roots in Indian knowledge systems in interaction with modern thought, to better understand the idea of India formulated by these three influential figures in history. The course includes detailed readings and discussions of some of their fundamental writings, focusing on specific themes and events from their lives.

## Course Outcomes (COs)

After successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

| CO | Description |
|---|---|
| CO1 | Understand the broader ethical, human responsibility of overriding importance while approaching scientific and technical challenges relating to building amenities and individual and corporate wealth |
| CO2 | Gain an introduction to traditional Indian Knowledge systems |
| CO3 | Understand that the thinkers studied shared a common view of life as an ongoing form of sacrifice beyond ritualistic practice, in a universe intricately connected in creation and destruction, where all human activity is part of this sacrifice and each individual develops with the support of others |
| CO4 | Recognize that understanding this fact is one of the highest functions of human consciousness — that one should enjoy one's social and economic advantage while being mindful that a portion of it needs to be shared, reflected in global ecological concerns, common ownership of resources, and limits on their use |

## Additional Notes

The source document elaborates at length on the intellectual-history framing of the course (comparing the evolution of ideas to organic growth and mutation, and to physical/mechanical limits), stating that the course examines how ideas evolve based on the genius of their proponents, interaction with historical forces, and human psychological traits — and how such an idea can become either a constructive or destructive force for humanity. It further notes that efforts are ongoing among communities to shape their destiny within a national scheme of identity and belonging, and that the foundational thinking of the 19th and 20th centuries regarding the modern Indian community may become relevant to imagining future communities amid today's challenges.
