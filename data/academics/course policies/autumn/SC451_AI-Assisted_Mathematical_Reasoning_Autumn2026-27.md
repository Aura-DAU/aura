## Course Description

Can AI help us discover, test, and verify mathematics? Modern AI tools can suggest conjectures, produce proof sketches, search for patterns, and help translate informal mathematical ideas into formal statements. But they can also make mistakes: they may hallucinate lemmas, miss assumptions, produce circular arguments, or generate convincing-looking proofs that are mathematically invalid. This course explores how to use AI critically, responsibly, and mathematically.

The course is organised around the workflow: Conjecture → Formalise → Verify → Critique → Refine. Students will learn how to move from examples and patterns to conjectures, from informal reasoning to formal statements, and from AI-generated suggestions to verified mathematical arguments. The course uses Lean 4 with mathlib4 as a formal verification environment. AI tools will be used as assistants for exploration, conjecture generation, proof sketching, formalisation, premise search, and proof repair — but not as unquestioned authorities.

**This is not a generic AI course.** Students should not take this course merely because AI is currently popular. It is not a course on generic AI usage, prompt shortcuts, chatbot tricks, AI trends, mathematics for AI, or automatic answer generation. The course does not treat AI as a substitute for mathematical thinking; a major aim is to show why AI-generated mathematical output must be questioned, checked, repaired, or rejected.

**Why this course matters:** AI is changing the way people search, write, compute, and reason. However, mathematics still demands correctness. A mathematical claim is not true because an AI model states it confidently; a proof is not valid because it sounds convincing; a Lean-like proof is not correct unless it actually compiles and verifies. This course asks: how can we use AI creatively without giving up mathematical rigour?

**Course theme:** AI can assist mathematical reasoning, but it cannot replace verification. Students will learn to use AI for generating examples, conjectures, proof ideas, counterexamples, formal statements, and possible premises, and to test these outputs through mathematical critique and, where appropriate, Lean-based verification.

## Course Outcomes (COs)

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

| CO | Description |
|---|---|
| CO1 | Use computational exploration to discover patterns in small mathematical structures |
| CO2 | Generate and refine conjectures using examples, search, heuristics, and AI tools |
| CO3 | Use prompting techniques for mathematical reasoning, and compare and critique AI-generated mathematical outputs |
| CO4 | Translate informal mathematical statements into precise formal statements, and write and check small proofs in Lean 4 |
| CO5 | Search for useful definitions, lemmas, and theorems in formal libraries |
| CO6 | Identify fabricated lemmas and invalid reasoning in AI-generated mathematical output, and repair or reject AI-generated proof attempts |
| CO7 | Understand the difference between AI-generated proof suggestions and formally verified proofs |
| CO8 | Complete a small end-to-end mathematical reasoning workflow using computation, AI assistance, and formal verification |

## Additional Notes

**Tools used (as stated in source):**
- *Core Tools*: Python for computational exploration; Lean 4 for formal proof verification; mathlib4 as a formal mathematics library; VS Code with the Lean extension; Python libraries such as NetworkX, NumPy, doctest, and pytest, as required.
- *AI-Assisted Tools* (depending on availability, institutional policy, and tool stability): LLM-based assistants for mathematical conjecture generation and proof sketching; AI tools for informal-to-formal translation; Lean-related AI tools such as Lean Copilot, LeanDojo, or similar systems; AI-supported search for relevant lemmas, definitions, or premises.

**Bring Your Own Device**: Students are required to bring their own laptop or suitable computing device to class, capable of running or accessing Python, VS Code, Lean 4 with the Lean extension, mathlib4, browser-based AI/LLM tools, and other browser-based or local tools required for course activities. Students should ensure their device is charged and ready for use, and bring a charger whenever possible. The instructor will provide guidance for installing or accessing required software, but students are responsible for maintaining a working setup.

**Who should take this course**: Students interested in mathematical reasoning and proof; AI for reasoning and discovery; formal verification; theorem proving; computational exploration of mathematical structures; responsible and critical use of AI; and understanding both the power and limitations of AI in mathematics.

**Who should think carefully before taking this course**: Students primarily looking for an easy elective; a course where AI tools generate most of the answers; a course focused mainly on popular AI applications; a course with minimal mathematics; a course without programming or tool setup; a course without formal proof; a course where attendance/participation are optional; or a course where vague explanations suffice.

**Attendance and Participation Policy**: This is a hands-on, discussion-based, tool-supported course involving live demonstrations, guided Lean 4 exercises, AI-assisted prompting, proof repair, hallucination detection, and in-class critique that cannot be fully replicated through notes, slides, or recordings. Students are expected to maintain at least 80% attendance across all scheduled sessions; attendance will be recorded regularly. Students who fall short may face, subject to applicable academic norms and instructor discretion: loss of marks in participation/lab/in-class-activity components; ineligibility to submit selected make-up work; requirement to complete additional compensatory assignments; non-consideration for borderline grade improvement; and/or restriction from participating in the final viva/demonstration component, where permitted by institute rules. Genuine medical, academic, or officially approved absences should be reported early with documentation and will be considered sympathetically, though students remain responsible for catching up. Mere physical presence without participation may not count as meaningful participation.

**Responsible Use of AI**: AI tools may be used for conjecture generation, proof sketching, counterexample search, informal-to-formal translation, premise search, proof repair, and comparison of mathematical explanations — but AI-generated outputs must not be treated as final answers and must be critically examined, corrected, verified, or rejected. Submitting AI-generated arguments, Lean code, or explanations without acknowledgement and reflection is treated as inappropriate academic practice. For relevant assignments/projects, students must maintain a brief AI-use/prompt log recording: the prompt/query used; the AI-generated response; what was accepted, modified, rejected, or corrected; whether the claim/proof was verified in Lean 4; and what mathematical issue, error, or misleading suggestion was identified.

**Collaboration and Group Work**: Some lab activities may be individual or in pairs, as announced. The capstone project may be individual or group-based depending on class size and topic complexity. Even in group work, each student must be able to explain the mathematical problem, conjectures considered, prompts used, AI outputs obtained, errors/incorrect AI output identified, formalisation attempted, Lean verification status, and final conclusions; individual viva/demonstration/reflection may be used to assess each student's contribution.

**Make-up Work**: Because many sessions involve live demonstrations and in-class critique, missed activities may not always be repeatable. Make-up work is not automatic and may be permitted only for genuine medical, official, or exceptional reasons with documentation, subject to instructor discretion.

**Capstone Project**: Students choose a small mathematical problem and carry out the full workflow: (1) explore examples computationally; (2) generate conjectures, possibly with AI assistance; (3) critique and refine the conjectures; (4) formulate precise mathematical statements; (5) attempt formal verification in Lean 4; (6) identify failed, incomplete, or hallucinated AI suggestions; (7) present what was verified, what failed, and what was learned. Possible domains include combinatorial games, graph theory, elementary number theory, finite discrete structures, and simple inductive mathematical systems. The final submission should include: problem description; computational exploration; AI-use/prompt log; informal proof attempts; formalisation attempts; Lean verification status; hallucination or error analysis; and final reflection.

**What success looks like (as stated in source)**: Success does not mean every proof attempt works immediately — failed prompts, wrong conjectures, incomplete formalisation attempts, Lean errors, and AI-generated nonsense are expected and are part of the course, not distractions from it. The aim is disciplined and rigorous mathematical reasoning in the presence of AI, not blind trust in AI.
