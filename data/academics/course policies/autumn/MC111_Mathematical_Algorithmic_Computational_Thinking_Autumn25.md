## Course Description

This course is an introduction to the fundamental modes of thought that underpin mathematics and computer science. Students will develop skills in mathematical, algorithmic, and computational thinking through an integrated curriculum of interactive puzzles, games, "maths magic," and hands-on activities. The goal is to build a powerful and versatile problem-solving toolkit by learning to think with precision, break down complex problems, identify patterns, and design logical solutions.

**Course Methodology:** This course is designed as a "thinking lab." All sessions are fully integrated and interactive. Every class is a dynamic mix of introducing new concepts and immediately exploring them through puzzles, strategic games, and collaborative problem-solving. Students are expected to come to every class prepared to actively participate, experiment with ideas, and engage in discussion.

## Course Structure

**Tentative Topical Outline (Integrated Approach):**

| Weeks | Module | Concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | The Art of Precise Instruction & Logical Deduction | Foundations of rigorous thinking; logical paradoxes; importance of precise instructions; deducing truths from constrained information |
| Weeks 3–4 | Thinking with Graphs and Networks | Modelling relationships; representing networks; finding efficient paths; principles of connectivity and structure |
| Weeks 5–6 | Order, Searching, and Algorithm Efficiency | Creating order from chaos; physically simulating and analysing sorting strategies, searching for items, measuring efficiency of methods |
| Week 7 | Mid-Semester Examination | — |
| Weeks 8–9 | Information, Representation, and Secret Sharing | How information is represented and secured; mathematics of secret-keeping through classic cryptography; data encoding for different purposes |
| Weeks 10–11 | Recursive Thinking and Strategic Gaming | Problems containing smaller versions of themselves (recursion); logic of game theory and winning strategies |
| Weeks 12–13 | Handling Complexity, Concurrency, and Infinity | Limits of computation; famously "hard" problems; challenges of simultaneous processes; mind-bending nature of infinite sets |
| Week 14 | Advanced Topics in Proof and Computation | Frontiers of logic, including zero-knowledge proofs; time dedicated to final projects |
| Finals Week | Final Group Project Due & End-Semester Examination | — |

## Final Group Project: Design an Unplugged Activity

**Project Goal:** Student groups (3–4 members) will synthesize the three modes of thinking by designing, documenting, and demonstrating an original "unplugged" activity, game, or puzzle that teaches a concept relevant to the course.

**Project Components and Deliverables:**
- **Public Exhibition (Group):** During the final week, all groups present their activity in a public exhibition, demonstrating the activity, engaging visitors, and explaining core concepts.
- **Activity Kit (Group):** After the exhibition, each group submits a complete, self-contained "activity kit" to SPARK, including all necessary physical materials and a clear instruction manual.
- **Group Report (Group):** A written report analysing the core principles of the project — rules of the activity, underlying mathematical/algorithmic/computational concepts, and a discussion of the design process.
- **Individual Reflection (Individual):** Each student submits an individual report exploring the principles of their group's project in greater depth (e.g., proposing generalizations, analysing restrictions/edge cases, connecting the core idea to other problems in mathematics and computer science).

**Project Grading Breakdown:**

| Component | Weightage |
|---|---|
| Public Exhibition & Demonstration | 30% |
| Activity Kit & Instructions | 20% |
| Group Report | 30% |
| Individual Reflection | 20% |
| **Total (of Final Project component)** | **100%** |

**Project Proposal Guidelines:** Each group must submit a 1-page project proposal for approval, including: (1) Project Title, (2) Group Members, (3) Core Concept, (4) Activity Overview, (5) Learning Objectives (2–3 specific items), (6) Preliminary Materials list, (7) Any Additional Information.

## Additional Notes

**Code of Conduct & Academic Integrity:** All students are expected to adhere to the highest standards of academic honesty and classroom conduct. Collaboration on in-class activities and puzzles is highly encouraged, but all submitted work (problem sets, write-ups, reports, examinations) must be the result of individual effort. Plagiarism — using another's ideas, words, or work without proper citation — will result at minimum in a score of zero for the assignment, and may be escalated to the university's disciplinary committee.

**Policy on the Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) Tools:**
- **Prohibited uses:** Generating solutions for any graded problem set, puzzle, or activity write-up; writing or generating any portion of individual or group reports; using AI tools during mid-semester or end-semester examinations. Submitting AI-generated work as one's own is treated as plagiarism.
- **Permitted & encouraged uses:** As a brainstorming partner (exploring related topics, alternative explanations); as an editing assistant (grammar/spelling/clarity checks on already-written reports); as a study tool (generating practice questions or quizzing on key terms).
- **Requirement for disclosure:** If an AI tool is used for permitted purposes, students must include a brief paragraph at the end of their submission detailing which tool was used and for what purpose.

**Instructor Communication Policy:** Best contacted via email; the instructor aims to respond within 48 hours on weekdays (weekend emails typically answered the following Monday). Students should sign off emails with their name.

**A Note on Discovery and Spoilers:** Students are strongly encouraged not to search for solutions to in-class activities or puzzles ahead of time, to preserve the collaborative discovery experience for themselves and classmates.

**"Food for Thought: The Spirit of Our Class"** — As a light-hearted addition, the course document includes an extensive, alphabetically-listed collection of student-brainstormed mottos/taglines describing the unique, unconventional spirit of the course (a "boot camp for your brain," puzzle- and game-based learning approach, etc.). This collection runs to well over 150 individual taglines contributed by previous students as a welcome gift to incoming students, celebrating themes such as recursion, logic puzzles, game theory, cryptography, and the "unplugged" (computer-free) approach to teaching computational thinking. Given the purely supplementary and non-substantive nature of this content (a celebratory list rather than course policy), it is summarized here rather than reproduced in full; the complete list is available in the source PDF.
