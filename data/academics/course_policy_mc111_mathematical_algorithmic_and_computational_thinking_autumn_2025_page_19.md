## Course Description

This course is an introduction to the fundamental modes of thought that underpin mathematics and computer science. We will develop skills in mathematical, algorithmic, and computational thinking through an integrated curriculum of interactive puzzles, games, “maths magic” and hands-on activities. The goal is to build a powerful and versatile problem-solving toolkit by learning to think with precision, break down complex problems, identify patterns, and design logical solutions. Course Methodology This course is designed as a "thinking lab." As noted in the class schedule, all sessions are full

## Evaluation / Grading Scheme

Your final grade is a comprehensive measure of individual mastery and collaborative application. It is determined by regular assignments, two examinations, a final group project, and in-class participation.  Weekly Problem Sets / Puzzle Solutions (15%)  Hands-on Activity Write-ups (15%)  Mid-Semester Examination (20%)  End-Semester Examination (20%)  Final Group Project (25%)  Active Participation (5%)

### Final Group Project:

Design an Unplugged Activity Project Goal: The goal of the final project is for student groups (3-4 members) to synthesize the three modes of thinking by designing, documenting, and demonstrating an original 'unplugged' activity, game, or puzzle that teaches a concept relevant to this course.

### Project Components and Deliverables:

- The project grade is a significant component of your final mark and is comprised of the following parts, which include both group and individual submissions:  Public Exhibition (Group): During the final week of the course, all groups will present their activity in a public exhibition. Your goal is to demonstrate the activity, engage visitors, and explain the core concepts it teaches.  Activity Kit (Group): After the exhibition, each group will submit a complete, self-contained "activity kit" to SPARK.

- This kit must include all necessary physical materials and a clear instruction manual so that others can use the activity in the future.  Group Report (Group): A written report analysing the core principles of the project. This report must detail the rules of the activity, the underlying mathematical, algorithmic, and computational concepts, and a discussion of your design process.

-  Individual Reflection (Individual): Each student will submit an individual report exploring the principles of their group's project in greater depth. This may include proposing generalizations, analysing potential restrictions or edge cases, or connecting the core idea to other problems in mathematics and computer science.

- Project Grading Breakdown: The final project component of your course grade will be calculated as follows: Public Exhibition & Demonstration: 30%  Activity Kit & Instructions: 20%  Group Report: 30%  Individual Reflection: 20%  Project Proposal Guidelines: Your group must submit a 1-page project proposal for approval. The goal is to formalize your idea and receive feedback before you begin the main phase of your work. Your proposal must include the following sections:.

- 1. Project Title: A creative and descriptive name for your activity, game, or puzzle.

- 2. Group Members: A list of all members of your project group.

- 3. Core Concept: A brief paragraph identifying the central concept from mathematics, computer science, or logic that your project will explore (e.g., graph colouring, recursive thinking, public-key cryptography, etc.).

- 4. Activity Overview: A summary of the activity itself. What will a participant do? What is the goal of your game or the solution to your puzzle? How does it demonstrate the core concept?

- 5. Learning Objectives: A list of 2-3 specific things a person should understand or be able to do after completing your activity.

- 6. Preliminary Materials: A short, bulleted list of the physical materials you anticipate needing to create your "activity kit."

Classroom Conduct: A productive learning environment is built on mutual respect. You are expected to actively participate in activities, engage in discussions thoughtfully, and listen to the perspectives of your peers and instructor. Academic Integrity & Plagiarism: While collaboration on in-class activities and puzzles is highly encouraged, all submitted work—including problem sets, write- ups, reports, and examinations—must be the result of your own individual effort and understanding. Plagiarism is defined as the act of using another person's ideas, words, or work and presenting them as your own without proper citation or acknowledgement. This includes copying from online sources, other students, or previous course materials. Any instance of academic dishonesty will be taken very seriously and will result, at a minimum, in a score of zero for the assignment and may be escalated to the university's disciplinary committee. When in doubt about how to properly cite or collaborate, please ask the instructor. Policy on the Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) Tools Our Guiding Philosophy: The primary goal of this course is to develop your unique mathematical, algorithmic, and computational thinking skills. The assignments are designed to be the practice ground for this development. Therefore, our policy on AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, etc., is designed to protect this core objective. Prohibited Uses: The following uses of AI tools are strictly forbidden and will be treated as a violation of academic integrity: Generating solutions for any graded problem set, puzzle, or activity write-  up. Writing or generating any portion of your individual or group reports.  Using AI tools in any capacity during the mid-semester or end-semester  examinations. Submitting work generated by an AI as your own is a form of plagiarism and will be subject to the consequences outlined in the Code of Conduct. Permitted & Encouraged Uses: You are permitted and even encouraged to use AI tools in the following ways to support your learning: As a brainstorming partner: To explore related topics or ask for  alternative explanations of a concept we've discussed in class (e.g., "Can you explain the concept of recursion using an analogy other than the Tower of Hanoi?"). As an editing assistant: To check for grammar, spelling, and clarity on a  report you have already written. As a study tool: To generate practice questions or quiz yourself on key  terms and definitions. Requirement for Disclosure: If you use an AI tool for any of the permitted purposes on an assignment, you must include a brief paragraph at the end of your submission detailing which tool you used and for what purpose. Transparency is key to responsible use. A Final Word of Caution: You are responsible for the correctness and integrity of everything you submit. AI tools can and do make mistakes. Relying on them for understanding rather than for support will hinder your learning and likely impact your performance. Assignment Submission Policy All assignments, including problem sets and activity write-ups, must be submitted electronically through the Moodle LMS. To ensure fairness and keep all students on the same schedule, late submissions for problem sets and activity write-ups will not be accepted. To provide flexibility and account for illness, emergencies, or other life events, I will automatically drop your lowest scores at the end of the semester. For example, if we have 10 problem sets, only your best 8 scores will count toward your final grade (i.e., your lowest two scores will be dropped). The same principle will apply to the activity write-ups. Because this flexible policy is in place, no individual extensions will be granted. It is your responsibility to manage your submissions accordingly. Instructor Communication Policy The best way to contact me is via email. I will do my best to respond within 48 hours on weekdays. Emails sent over the weekend will typically be answered on the following Monday. Ensure that you sign-off your emails with your name. Tentative Topical Outline (Integrated Approach) Our journey is structured around core thinking skills. Each week, we will use interactive puzzles, games, and challenges as a vehicle to explore these powerful concepts. Weeks 1-2: The Art of Precise Instruction & Logical Deduction  Concepts: We will explore the foundations of rigorous thinking, investigating logical paradoxes, the importance of precise instructions, and how to deduce truths from constrained information. Weeks 3-4: Thinking with Graphs and Networks  Concepts: This module focuses on modelling relationships. We will explore how to represent networks, find efficient paths, and understand the principles of connectivity and structure through hands-on challenges. Weeks 5-6: Order, Searching, and Algorithm Efficiency  Concepts: How do we create order out of chaos? We will physically simulate and analyse various strategies for sorting information, searching for items, and measuring the efficiency of our methods. Week 7: Mid-Semester Examination  Weeks 8-9: Information, Representation, and Secret Sharing  Concepts: This module explores how information is represented and secured. We will delve into the mathematics of secret-keeping through classic cryptography and learn how data can be encoded for different purposes. Weeks 10-11: Recursive Thinking and Strategic Gaming  Concepts: We will tackle problems that contain smaller versions of themselves, exploring the elegant and powerful idea of recursion. We will also investigate the logic of game theory and how to identify winning strategies. Weeks 12-13: Handling Complexity, Concurrency, and Infinity  Concepts: Here we confront the limits of computation. We will grapple with famously "hard" problems, the challenges of simultaneous processes, and the mind-bending nature of infinite sets through famous thought experiments. Week 14: Advanced Topics in Proof and Computation  Concepts: Our final module explores the frontiers of logic, including how it's possible to prove you know a secret without revealing the secret itself, and we will dedicate time to developing your final projects. Finals Week: Final Group Project Due & End-Semester Examination  A Note on Discovery and Spoilers: The magic of this course comes from live, collaborative problem-solving. The puzzles and activities are designed to be tackled with fresh eyes. To preserve this experience for yourself and your classmates, you are strongly encouraged not to search for the solutions to any in- class activities or puzzles ahead of time or until asked to do so. The goal is not just to find the answer, but to experience the journey of discovery.
