# HM412 World Literature in Short Fiction (HM412)

## Overview

This document presents the detailed policy and syllabus structure for the course HM412 World Literature in Short Fiction (HM412). It includes details on course objectives, credits, prerequisites, syllabus content, evaluation schemes, and contact information where available.

## Main Content

HM412 World Literature in Short Fiction

(Winter 2026)

Instructor: Satvik Gupta, 3204, Faculty Block-3

Course Placement: HASS Elective/Open Elective, B.Tech. (Semester - VI)

Course format: 3 Lecture hours

Course content: “The experience of reading world literature is a form of travel—a mode of 
cultural encounter that presents us with languages, cultural norms, customs and ideas that may 
be unfamiliar to us, even strange. As readers, each time we begin to read a new work, we put 
ourselves in the role of a traveller in a foreign land, trying to understand its practices and 
values and hoping to feel, to some degree and in some way, connected to and welcome among 
the people we meet there” (The Norton Anthology of World Literature).

Following an introductory discourse on the history and overarching tradition of world 
literature, this course will limit its scope to the texts produced during the nineteenth and 
twentieth centuries. This period of world history bore witness to radical change and profound 
upheaval, marked by world wars, the fall of empires, the rise of new ideologies, and 
unprecedented technological advancements. This course, therefore, explores how authors 
across the globe utilised the short story form to capture the anxieties and transformations of the 
world around them. The readings are organised into four thematic modules that position the 
‘modern individual’ against the bureaucratic city, the legacy of the empire, linguistic labyrinths 
and the frailty of borders. Through this eclectic mix of narratives, learners are expected to 
cultivate a deeper appreciation for the short story form as a global medium of artistic 
expression.

Note: This course demands rigorous reading. Students will be required to read all the primary 
texts mentioned below. Furthermore, the attendance benchmark for this course is 60%; 
attendance below this threshold will result in a reduction of the grade.

Course Objectives:

This course aims to:

●​ Situate key works of short fiction within their broader historical and cultural contexts. 
●​ Explore the development of major literary movements through representative texts. 
●​ Cultivate skills in close reading and critical analysis. 
●​ Present a diverse selection of short fiction from various global traditions to highlight the 
richness of modern world literature.

Course Syllabus:

Primary readings are the essential texts that will be discussed during the semester. Additional 
readings will be referenced in the classroom, but they aren’t included in the overall evaluation and 
are provided here for your convenience and reference. All the texts mentioned below will be 
provided to the students before each lecture.

Introduction:

David Damrosch, “Goethe Coins a Phrase” in What Is World Literature (2003).

Module One: Modernity and the Bureaucratic City

Nikolai Gogol, “The Overcoat” (Russia, 1842)

Herman Melville, “Bartleby, the Scrivener” (USA, 1853)

Module Two: Empire and the Private Sphere

Rabindranath Tagore, “Punishment” (India, 1892)

Katherine Mansfield, “The Garden Party” (New Zealand, 1922)

Doris Lessing, “The Old Chief Mshlanga” (Southern Rhodesia/Zimbabwe, 1951)

Module Three: Labyrinths of Meaning – Language and Translation

Ryūnosuke Akutagawa, “In a Bamboo Grove” (Japan, 1922)

Jorge Luis Borges, “The Garden of Forking Paths” (Argentina, 1941)

Vladimir Nabokov, “Signs and Symbols” (USA/Russia, 1948)

Module Four: Revolution, Partition, and the Border

Lu Xun, “Diary of a Madman” (China, 1918)

Saadat Hasan Manto, “Toba Tek Singh” (India, 1955)

Albert Camus, “The Guest” (France/Algeria, 1957)

3. Group Activities: 20%

Course Outcomes:   
  Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

●​ Connect literary works to their broader historical and cultural contexts. 
●​ Compare and contrast literary styles and movements, such as Modernism, Realism, Magical 
Realism, and Postcolonialism. 
●​ Develop informed and nuanced arguments about global literary traditions. 
●​ Cultivate an appreciation for diverse literary voices and styles from around the world.
