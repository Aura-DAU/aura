Course Outline 
 
This course is an introduction, within a social scientific framework, to Indian social organization with 
special reference to its social forms and their genealogies. The course begins by foregrounding the 
emergence of India as a civilizational imaginary and nation-state within the narrative-historical 
framework of the region. The aim is to unpack the idea of the “unity” of India given the pluralism and 
diversity it ostensibly accommodates. 
 
Historically, India has been interpreted as a nation of communities with well-established communal 
ways of living. This is reflected in its texts, habits of life, traditions, agrarian practices, social 
organization, political behaviour and several other aspects of social life. However, the advent of 
modernity has profoundly changed South Asian society. Colonial modernity has catalyzed socially and 
politically important changes in its social fabric, thus raising important questions. Was India always a 
region with fixed boundaries and identity, a stable and constant social organization, and settled 
communal modes of life? If so, in what ways pre-modern India was different from the present one? 
What sort of changes did colonialism bring about in Indian society? Is there an inherent civilizational 
dynamic to social life in India which is markedly different from the one that is found in Western 
societies? Did the nation-state India flatten out all of its myriad forms of diversity—communities, 
languages, cultures, lifestyles—and their historical origins? Or, did the social forms in India find new 
modes of articulation in conditions of modernity? More importantly, how is the history of the present 
to be understood because of what happened in South Asia in the last few centuries? 
 
The course aims to find answers to these questions with the help of the decades-long social scientific 
scholarship now available. It would cover several relevant topics, such as colonial law and state, the 
formation of the public, colonial knowledge, village India, agrarian economy, peasant histories, caste 
and social structure, modernization, family and kinship, nationalism, communalism, and globalization. 
 
 
Grading: 
 
First In- Semester Examination - 30% 
Second In-Semester Examination - 30% 
Group Assignment - 40% 
 
Course Objective: 
 
This course aims to construct a comparative framework for the understanding of different cultures with 
particular reference to social organization, politics, 
religion and symbolism illustrated with various ethnographic examples. The course is designed to 
provide with the means to apply basic anthropological understandings of society and culture in the 
analysis of meanings, actions and explanations that is the basis for communication in the society. 
Students will be expected to reflect upon the Indian society utilizing the readings and lectures.
