## Section 2 — What Is Workplace Sexual Harassment

Defines "workplace" broadly (covering both organised and unorganised sectors, government and private, and locations visited in the course of employment, including transport and even a dwelling place for domestic workers). Defines sexual harassment per the Act's five categories (unwelcome physical contact/advances, demands for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and other unwelcome sexual conduct). Emphasizes that harassment is a subjective experience — impact matters more than the harasser's intent, and it typically occurs within a power imbalance. Provides illustrative example behaviours (from remarks and jokes through stalking and physical contact), examples of retaliatory/undermining conduct that may also indicate harassment, and examples of ordinary workplace management actions that do **not** constitute harassment. Distinguishes "quid pro quo" harassment (sexual favours tied to employment/academic outcomes) from "hostile work environment" harassment, illustrated with several composite (fictional) case scenarios.

## Section 3 — Prevention & Prohibition

Defines who counts as an "employer" and "Appropriate Government" under the Act, and the role of the District Officer (a designated District Magistrate/Collector-level official) in areas without a formal employer structure. Sets out employer/District Officer responsibilities: creating and publicising a policy, ensuring awareness training, constituting Complaints Committees, training them, and annual reporting. Describes the two complaint mechanisms mandated by the Act:

- **Internal Complaints Committee (ICC):** mandatory at every workplace, chaired by a senior woman employee, with at least 2 further members and an external NGO/legal-background member; must be at least 50% women.
- **Local Complaints Committee (LCC):** constituted by the District Officer for unorganised-sector workers, small establishments (fewer than 10 workers), domestic workers, or complaints against the employer personally.

Cites case law establishing that failure to constitute a Complaints Committee can itself result in significant financial liability for an employer.

## Section 4 — Redress *(the section referenced by the companion "Procedure to file a complaint" pointer document)*

- **Who can complain, and where:** Workers at workplaces with fewer than 10 employees generally complain to the LCC (via a Nodal Officer); most other employees complain to their workplace's ICC.
- **What a complaint should contain:** A written description of the incident(s) with dates, timing, location, the respondent's identity, and the working relationship between the parties; assistance in writing the complaint must be offered if requested.
- **What a complainant/employee can expect:** A trained, competent Committee; a time-bound process; confidentiality; protection from retaliation; and support if criminal proceedings are pursued. Both complainant and respondent have defined rights — a fair hearing, access to the evidence and witness list submitted by the other party, confidentiality of identity, and a right of appeal.
- **Committee responsibilities and required skills:** preparation, legal knowledge, evidence-gathering, interviewing, impartial analysis, and clear reporting.
- **Do's and don'ts for the Committee**, and a list of "non-negotiables" during an inquiry (details in the original document).
- **The full complaint process — six stages, fifteen steps:**
  1. **Stage One (Steps 1–5):** Receive/acknowledge the complaint; explore informal vs. formal resolution; attempt informal conciliation only at the complainant's request and never involving direct complainant–respondent negotiation or monetary settlement; move to the formal mechanism if warranted; notify the respondent in writing within 7 days and allow 10 days for their written response.
  2. **Stage Two — Planning (Steps 6–7):** Build a confidential case file, review the applicable law/policy, list relevant dates/events/witnesses, and prepare an interview plan. Also covers available interim measures (e.g., transfer, leave up to 3 months, restricting the respondent from supervising or evaluating the complainant) and ongoing support (counselling, communication, leave).
  3. **Stage Three — Interviews (Steps 8–9):** Interview the complainant, witnesses, and respondent separately and confidentially — the two parties are not to be brought face-to-face — then assess whether enough information has been gathered.
  4. **Stage Four — Reasoning (Steps 10–12):** Weigh the evidence on a balance-of-probabilities standard, confirm the conduct meets the legal definition of harassment, build a timeline, and compare witness statements for consistency.
  5. **Stage Five — Finding & Recommendation (Steps 13–14):** Reach a finding (upheld / not upheld / inconclusive), sharing it with both parties for a chance to respond before finalising if both are employees. Recommendations may range from no action, to disciplinary measures (apology, reprimand, warning, withheld promotion/raise, termination, counselling, community service), to financial damages assessed against factors like trauma, medical costs, career impact, and the respondent's financial standing.
  6. **Stage Six — Report (Step 15):** A final written report documenting the complaint, process, evidence, analysis, findings, and recommendations.

- **Key statutory timelines:**
  | Milestone | Deadline |
  |---|---|
  | Submission of complaint | Within 3 months of the last incident |
  | Notice to respondent | Within 7 days of complaint receipt |
  | Completion of inquiry | Within 90 days |
  | Submission of Committee report | Within 10 days of inquiry completion |
  | Implementation of recommendations | Within 60 days |
  | Appeal | Within 90 days of the recommendations |

- **Confidentiality:** The Act prohibits disclosing the complaint's contents, the identities of complainant/respondent/witnesses, and details of proceedings or recommendations, subject to specified penalties for breach (a fine, or as prescribed by applicable Service Rules), with a narrow exception allowing disclosure of case outcomes without identifying details.

## Section 5 — Monitoring

Describes the Appropriate Government's (Central or State) authority to inspect workplaces and records; the requirement for ICCs/LCCs to submit Annual Reports (numbers of complaints received/disposed/pending, awareness activities, and action taken) up through the District Officer to the State Government; and penalties for employers who fail to constitute a Committee, fail to act on its recommendations, fail to report, or otherwise contravene the Act — up to a stated monetary penalty, doubling on repeat violations, and potential loss of business registration/licensing.

## Section 6 — Global Norms and Good Practices

Situates the Act within international human-rights frameworks (CEDAW recommendations, the UN Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women, the Beijing Platform for Action, and ILO conventions on domestic workers), framing workplace sexual harassment as a human-rights and equality issue rather than purely a labour-law matter. Closes with six practical recommendations for workplaces: effectively communicate the policy, publicise complaint channels, run orientation/training, maintain a well-trained Committee, encourage leadership role-modelling, and build an inclusive culture involving both men and women.
